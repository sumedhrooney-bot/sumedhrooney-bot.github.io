Aspirations, Investment, and the Inverted U (AK and CRRA, ratio form)

Sumedh Ramakrishna | Alfred Weber Institute, Heidelberg University | November 2025
Behavioral Economics Reference Dependent Utility Loss Aversion AK Growth Model IFLS Panel Microdata R / Stata

The Budget Constraint

Following the work of Ray and Genicot, from a behavioural perspective I am interested in the implications of aspirations on decision making and hence in my simplistic model I take aspirations as a given scalar. I assume each household has current income \(y > 0\). In a single period, the household chooses to allocate income between consumption \(c\) and investment \(k\), which will then yield \(z = f(k)\) in the next period. The budget constraint is given by:

$$y = c + k, \quad k \ge 0, \quad f'(k) > 0, \quad f''(k) \le 0$$

Aspirations and Preferences

Let \(a\) be the target that the household aspires to reach for the next period. We define a reference dependent utility function around \(a\). The utility of the household depends on three things:

  • Current consumption
  • The outcome of the next period \(f(k)\)
  • A gain loss utility term referenced around \(a\) which is my aspirational component that rewards exceeding \(a\) and penalizes falling short of \(a\)
$$U(c, k, a) = u(c) + \delta [w_0(f(k)) + \pi_G W_G((f(k) - a)_+) - \pi_L W_L((a - f(k))_+)]$$

Here, \(u\), \(w_0\), \(W_G\), and \(W_L\) are all increasing concave functions, \(\delta > 0\) is the weight on how much we value future outcomes today, and \(\pi_G, \pi_L \ge 0\) gives us the strength of gains and the strength of losses where \((x)_+ = \max\{x, 0\}\).

Ratio Normalization (AK, CRRA) and Solution

Since we are interested in studying the effect that aspirations have on investment, similar to Ray and Genicot 2017, I too use the common elasticity growth model from their paper where I start with imposing an AK setting making the production function linear as they argue that this formulation also allows for exogenous labour income in every period. This formulation implies \(f(k) = \rho k = z\) with \(\rho > 1\). This means investing \(k\) yields \(\rho k\) in the next period. Next, I make the following assumptions below such that utilities are constant elasticity with the same elasticity for each of them:

$$u(c) = c^{1-\sigma}, \quad w_0(z) = \delta z^{1-\sigma}, \quad W_G(e) = \delta \pi_G e^{1-\sigma}, \quad W_L(e) = \delta \pi_L e^{1-\sigma}, \quad \sigma \in (0, 1)$$

Here \(\sigma \in (0, 1)\) is the common curvature. A lower \(\sigma\) would imply more elastic responses. \(\delta > 0\) is the discount weight on next period payoffs as viewed today. \(\pi_G > 0\) is the weight on the additional utility boost we receive from beating our aspiration target and \(\pi_L > 0\) is the negative utility penalty we receive for falling short of our aspiration target. Typically \(\pi_L > \pi_G\) due to loss aversion. \(e \ge 0\) denotes the gap with \(e = (z - a)_+\) and \(e = (a - z)_+\) for the gain and loss respectively and not to forget \((x)_+ = \max\{x, 0\}\).

Finally, I define the growth factor given by \(g = z / y\), the aspirations ratio given by \(r = a / y\), and the investment share given by \(i = k / y = g / \rho\). After imposing all these conditions on our utility function for the household, we get the following reduced equation, which is easy to work with:

$$\tilde{U}(g, r) = \left(1 - \frac{g}{\rho}\right)^{1-\sigma} + \delta [g^{1-\sigma} + \pi_G (g - r)_+^{1-\sigma} - \pi_L (r - g)_+^{1-\sigma}], \quad g \in [0, \rho]$$

In this equation above, once the household chooses an aspiration target \(a\), their aspirations ratio \(r\) is fixed and they choose their level of investment \(z\) to maximize their Utility function through their growth rate \(g\). Since our Utility function is piecewise we can partition it into two zones. I call them the satisfied and frustrated zone based on the level of investment \(z\). The satisfied zone is one where the chosen \(z\) makes \(g \ge r\) and the frustrated zone is one where it makes \(g < r\). This gives rise to two First Order Conditions piecewise in \(g\) and is given by:

$$\text{Satisfied } (g \ge r): \quad \left(1 - \frac{g}{\rho}\right)^{-\sigma} = \delta \rho [g^{-\sigma} + \pi_G (g - r)^{-\sigma}]$$ $$\text{Frustrated } (g < r): \quad \left(1 - \frac{g}{\rho}\right)^{-\sigma} = \delta \rho [g^{-\sigma} + \pi_L (r - g)^{-\sigma}]$$

There exists \(r^* > 1\) such that \(g^*(r)\) solves the satisfied first order condition for \(r < r^*\) and the frustrated first order condition for \(r > r^*\). In the satisfied region, \(\partial g / \partial r\) is positive while in the frustrated region it is negative. Thus \(g^*(r)\) is increasing in \(r\) until \(r^*\) and then decreasing in \(r\) after yielding an inverted U shape. If we look at investment \(k\) keeping income constant, it would yield this inverted U shape too as \(g = z / y\) and \(f(k) = \rho k = z\):

$$k^*(y, a) = \frac{y}{\rho} g^*\left(\frac{a}{y}\right), \quad i^*(r) = \frac{g^*(r)}{\rho}$$
